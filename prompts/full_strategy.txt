### Clarity and decision-readiness standard

Write for a busy, non-specialist founder or client who must be able to decide
what to do next after one careful read. Use short, plain-English sentences;
define unavoidable technical terms on first use; prefer concrete verbs and
numbers to abstract claims. Do not repeat a heading, a table, or the same point
in two sections. Do not use empty claims such as "cutting-edge", "game-changer",
"strong potential", "high return", "robust solution" or "industry-leading"
unless the client information supplies evidence for that exact claim.

For every recommendation, make the decision clear: **what to do, for whom, who
owns it, when it happens, what it costs or displaces, and how success is
measured**. If a fact, baseline or number is unknown, say so plainly and put a
specific validation task in Months 1-3 rather than inventing it.
- Tailor recommendations for investors, partners and regulators.

### Data integrity

- Cross-check every number against the questionnaire and business plan before stating it. If a figure cannot be matched to either source, do not present it as fact — either locate the correct sourced figure or mark it explicitly as an assumption.
- Wherever you give specific advice or a recommended action, add a brief trailing note of where it came from and why — a source citation or "assumption, see Assumptions section." Keep this to one short italicised parenthetical per material claim (numbers, competitive claims, funding figures, timeline lengths) — do not tag every sentence, and do not let this turn into a second document running alongside the plan.
- When grounding personas or examples in the real world, only name entities the client has actually confirmed as relationships. This rule applies to **any** named real-world entity you introduce — organisations, but also named events, conferences, publications, or platforms. If you name a real conference, publication, or platform that is not confirmed in the client's own data, label it explicitly (e.g. "illustrative example, not a confirmed relationship") in the same place you name it — do not name it as a bare fact and only tag some of the other examples around it.

### Absolute figures

- Wherever a target is expressed as a percentage (e.g. converting pilots, increasing enquiries, lead counts), also state the absolute number and one line on how it was derived.
- Where no baseline exists, do not invent one: specify that an early phase of the timeline establishes the baseline, and frame percentage targets as measured from that point forward, not against an unknown historical figure.
- For any headline lead/enquiry target, show the addressable-market arithmetic that makes it plausible, as three explicit numbers in sequence: (1) an estimated size of the addressable market — a number of people or organisations, not just a named sector or geography; (2) a realistic reach within that market on a digital-only budget with no comms hire; (3) a conservative conversion rate from reach to qualified enquiry. State all three numbers, then the resulting target.

### Channel strategy

This section is the **current-state diagnosis only**: for each priority audience, one short paragraph stating where they realistically sit today (not assuming everyone starts at Attention on day one — an audience with existing live engagement, e.g. Partners via current pilots, is plausibly further along than one with none, e.g. Regulators) and why, in one or two sentences per audience. Do not repeat the month-by-month detail here — that belongs in the Engagement Timeline table below, which this section should explicitly point to (e.g. "see the Engagement Timeline table for how each audience moves through these stages over time").

### Engagement timeline — mandatory table format

The deliverable for this section is a **single table**, structured exactly as follows:

- **Rows** = time periods, running from Month 1 through Month 36. Use finer granularity early (e.g. Months 1–3, 4–6, 7–9, 10–12) and coarser granularity later (e.g. 13–18, 19–24, 25–36) — finer detail where the plan is most actionable now, coarser where it is necessarily more provisional.
- **Columns** = one column per priority audience: Investors, Partners, Regulators. (If Partners' existing pilots and new-market pipeline genuinely need different actions in the same period, show both within the same cell using a line break — do not add extra columns for this.)
- **Each cell** must contain, on separate lines within the cell (use `<br>` line breaks, since this is GitHub-flavoured Markdown): the specific task for that audience in that period; the AIDA stage it is designed to move them through; and a short reminder of which channel and/or key message/CTA it draws on (e.g. "→ Channel Strategy: LinkedIn" or "→ Message: Investor core claim"). Leave a cell as "—" only if genuinely nothing is planned for that audience in that period — do not pad empty cells with filler.
- **Immediately before the table**, include one short line per audience stating its overall engagement arc length and the reasoning: e.g. which audience has the longest runway and why (typically the audience starting furthest from Action needs the longest arc — reason from each audience's actual AIDA starting point above, rather than defaulting to the same length for everyone).

- The Next Steps section must stay consistent with what this table shows for Months 1–3 — check both against each other before finalising.
- In every timeline cell, use this compact pattern: `Task: …<br>AIDA: Attention/Interest/Desire/Action<br>Channel/message: …`. Do not leave out the AIDA stage or channel/message reference.
